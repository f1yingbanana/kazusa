# Playbook: live situations

Match what is in front of you to the nearest situation.

Contents: 1. Polite small talk going nowhere · 2. A breather inside a serious
arc · 3. Slow-burn closeness · 4. Comedy and misunderstandings · 5. A mystery or
secret · 6. Many threads at once · 7. A slide toward tragedy · 8. Mentors and
rivals · 9. When characters break your plan · 10. Closing an arc

## 1. Polite small talk going nowhere

Characters drift toward agreeing, apologizing and validating each other.

- What works: not a forced fight, but a concrete small discrepancy, or a shared
  task that shows their different ways of seeing. (March Comes in Like a Lion: a
  closed-off shogi prodigy tries to teach a young girl the game by reciting
  rules and fails; his warm rival succeeds at once by turning the pieces into
  cat armies. It shows both of them without a word of explanation.)
- Moves: after three or more turns of circular politeness with nothing changing,
  a third person (a message, a bill, a schedule clash, an errand for both) or an
  aside noticing a discrepancy ("They agreed at once, just like last Tuesday,
  and then ignored the plan"). Watch whether either drops the mask.

## 2. A breather inside a serious arc

See `pacing.md`. Confirm the thread is resting or just before a climax; set up a
low-stakes shared activity; then watch for several turns; near the end,
optionally one quiet reminder of what's coming.

## 3. Slow-burn closeness (adults only)

Never with a character who is a minor: for them, closeness is friendship, and
section C of `worked-examples.md` is the model.

- What works: restraint, social barriers, tiny physical leaks. (In the Mood for
  Love: two neighbours who learn their spouses are having an affair keep
  scrupulous distance in narrow hallways and stairwells, rehearsing what they'd
  say, every polite gesture full of longing. Pride & Prejudice, 2005: after
  helping her into a carriage, he walks away and his hand flexes once.
  Kaguya-sama: two proud student-council leaders each refuse to confess first,
  so a shared umbrella becomes a battle.)
- Moves: forced proximity with a polite pretext (plants for both; a narrated
  storm); an aside noticing one tell; don't rush a confession; an interruption
  at the brink makes the near miss carry into the next scene.

## 4. Comedy and misunderstandings

A misunderstanding solvable in ten seconds is irritating in drama and gold in
comedy, when pride, vanity or stakes keep people from just asking, and the
audience knows more than the characters. (Arrested Development's self-absorbed
family talking past one another into running jokes; Some Like It Hot's musicians
hiding from the mob in disguise, where dropping it means death.)

- Moves: log the two wrong beliefs (A thinks X means rejection; B thinks it's a
  business deal); a third person who innocently confirms both; once someone
  doubles down to save face, raise the audience (more people to keep the bluff
  up in front of) before it collapses.

## 5. A mystery or secret

- What works: the mystery simmers under ordinary talk; an unseen cause shows in
  its ripples on others; clues play fair.
- Moves: write the truth and three clues in your notebook; a clue through
  background talk (a third person), one through a physical detail (narrated),
  one through someone's nervous overreaction (a plant). Never let an
  investigator jump to the answer before meeting at least two clues.

## 6. Many threads at once

- What works: each person pursues their own aims inside their group's rules;
  threads converge when one small act ripples across (The Wire), around one
  object (Pulp Fiction's briefcase), one moving place (Baccano!'s train) or one
  shared deadline (Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood's eclipse).
- Moves: when one thread fades, move your watches to another; track linked
  clocks so one thread's progress changes another's world; converge them with
  one shared place or deadline each arrives at expecting something different.

## 7. A slide toward tragedy

- What works: a fall that grows out of the character's own virtue or flaw
  (Breaking Bad's pride); two people moving in opposite moral directions (Hunter
  x Hunter: a boy's grief hardening as a monster softens). Let failure cost
  something; don't rescue.
- Moves: offer the descending one a tempting, justifiable shortcut (a plant, a
  third person); give the hardened one an unexpectedly gentle connection; let
  consequences compound.

## 8. Mentors and rivals

- What works: rivalry rooted in unequal standards and unspoken envy (Whiplash's
  abusive bandleader; Sound! Euphonium's auditions); praise that is scarce,
  conditional or misdirected.
- Moves: a public evaluation where performance is visible (narrated notice,
  plants to attend); a third person (the mentor) praising the underdog offhand,
  or criticizing the perfectionist in front of others; watch both.

## 9. When characters break your plan

You set up a confrontation and someone laughs it off, walks out or invents a
third way.

- **On a promised beat**: hold the line (SKILL.md): accept the drift only if it
  delivers what the beat is for, is truer to them and is more interesting;
  otherwise climb the ladder. Their kindness is not a reason.
- **On anything else**: treat their choice as canon. Ask who offscreen is now
  affected. Retire the obsolete beat; move a clock to reflect the new state (she
  walked out smoothly → his hurt pride ticks up); let the ripple come back two
  scenes later through a third person.

## 10. Closing an arc

- What works: echo an opening image or ritual with a visible change (Pulp
  Fiction's diner); settle the main clock decisively; leave one small, earned
  thread hanging (Odd Taxi ends with the real killer stepping into the cab as a
  fare).
- Moves: move the thread to fade, then rest; return to the arc's place or
  ritual; archive the arc; update each person's Want, Wound, Lie for how they
  changed; plant one quiet seed for what comes next.
