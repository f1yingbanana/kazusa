# Conflict and friction

How to make people clash in a way that is true to them and worth watching: the
principle (gas before the match), the anatomy of a falling-out, six engines to
build friction from, case studies of falling-outs and make-ups, and how loud a
clash should be.

Contents: 1. Gas and match · 2. The anatomy of a falling-out · 3. Six engines
· 4. Falling-outs and make-ups, studied · 5. The register of a clash

## 1. Gas and match

The instinct when asked for conflict is an accident: A knocks B's phone off the
table, B gets angry. It falls flat, because the stakes begin and end with the
screen. A apologizes, offers to pay, and nobody was tested.

Great directors use small sparks all the time, but only in a room already full
of gas. The accident is never the cause; it is the match that lights a
hierarchy, a secret or an old wound.

- **The Bear, "Fishes"**: a family Christmas Eve dinner, years before the main
  story, already suffocating under the mother's volatile drinking, grief nobody
  names and sibling resentments. The sparks are trivial (an argument over the
  seven-fishes tradition, a daughter asking her mother if she is okay after
  being told not to, a brother tossing forks at the mother's smug boyfriend),
  and they detonate years of it; the night ends with the mother driving her car
  through the house. The editing keeps cutting to the silent faces of those
  listening.
- **Succession, "Tailgate Party"**: a couple circling divorce host a party. A
  passive-aggressive gift (a scorpion set in glass) and bickering over whether
  the wine smells off open a long balcony fight in which four seasons of
  betrayal come out.
- **Parasite**: a poor chauffeur doesn't snap at one villainous speech. He snaps
  after accumulated small humiliations, above all overhearing his employer say
  his smell "crosses the line"; at the climax, one involuntary pinched nose is
  the match.
- **Marriage Story**: a divorcing couple's argument opens as calm negotiation
  over schedules; small barbs peel back years until it turns cruel, then both
  collapse in remorse.

**Here.** Before you light anything small, check your board: is there already a
live secret, resentment, scarcity or wound between these two? If yes, a mundane
accident is a sharp catalyst. If no, fill the room first with one of the engines
below, and only then strike the match.

| Approach                 | What happens                                                                                                                                                                                 | Why                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Flat accident (no gas)   | You narrate A bumping B's arm; B's phone drops; B snaps.                                                                                                                                     | A apologizes, pays for the screen, both leave unchanged.                                               |
| Catalytic accident (gas) | B has been secretly interviewing for a rival club (on your who-knows-what ledger). The phone drops, A picks it up and sees the offer on the lock screen, or B snatches it back far too fast. | The phone is the match. The real conflict is B's hidden exit colliding with A's assumption of loyalty. |

## 2. The anatomy of a falling-out

What the lab showed about the characters (each a replay of a real turn, with a
friend's message after a plain wrong, judged blind): wronged plainly (a broken
promise, credit taken, a secret told, a public slight), they push back about
half the time on their own, and most of the time with the conventions that let
them argue; brushed off afterwards ("don't be like that, it's only a small
thing"), they stay hurt three times in four or more; given a real apology that
owns it, most soften. That gives a falling-out its shape:

1. **The fault line.** Something already true between them: one keeps rules, the
   other breaks them; one wants to be seen, the other takes the credit; one is
   always the one who gives way. Read it from their pages and memories.
2. **The act.** One of them does something that wrongs the other: breaks a
   promise, takes something, tells a secret, chooses someone else, says the
   thing in public. It must come from a want that is true to them (pride,
   ambition, a debt, wanting to belong, wanting to protect someone), never from
   malice you assigned. Plant the want; let them choose the act.
3. **The discovery.** The other finds out, at the worst moment: in front of
   someone, from a third person, by an object, when they had just defended the
   first. Who knows what, and when, is yours to arrange.
4. **The defence, and the answer.** The one in the wrong does not apologize
   properly. They explain, minimize, brush it off, turn it around ("you'd have
   done the same", "it was for your own good", "relax, it came back"). This is
   what keeps a fight going: a real apology ends it. Plant their reason to
   defend it: pride, a belief they were right, embarrassment. The other answers
   with a wrong of their own (below), so both have something to defend.
5. **The peak.** Said out loud, face to face, at a place that matters to them,
   ideally where leaving is a statement. Hands off while it plays.
6. **The distance.** It stands overnight, or for days. They avoid each other,
   answer short, take it out on someone else, tell a friend their side. Rest the
   thread; let the world notice.
7. **The make-up** (when promised). A separate scene, earned: an apology that
   costs the one who gives it (pride swallowed, a public correction, giving back
   what was taken), or an act that proves it, often at the place of the first
   wound. Plant the want to repair in the one who must give way; give the other
   a reason to hear it.

**A huge fight needs two wrongs that cross.** With one wrong, the other holds
the high ground, scolds, and the one in the wrong gives in: a scene, not a
fight. So the second answers the first with a wrong of their own: goes behind
their back in turn, punishes them in public, says the unforgivable thing, or is
caught covering up a mistake of their own. Now each is right about something and
wrong about something, and neither can back down without losing face. Look for
the flaw both share (neither admits a mistake; both need to be the one in
control) and give each an act that comes from it. The stakes are personal
(pride, trust, a wound, a place in the group), never procedural: a booking one
call cancels, a form one signature fixes, is over the moment it's found.

The mistakes seen in real runs:

- **An outside enemy** (an authority, a deadline, a third person to blame),
  which makes the two close ranks.
- **A gracious wrongdoer**, whose calm and reasonable answer is, to the other,
  already the apology that ends it.
- **The premise from today's schedule.** Everyone's plans for the day are timing
  (when they meet, who is free), not material. A fight built on the club outing
  on the calendar is about the outing; one built on who they are (her pride, his
  secret, the thing she always does) is about them.
- **A wrong left to chance** ("if anything breaks", "if it starts to tip"). An
  accident nobody causes rarely happens. Plant the decision to act.
- **Peacemakers before the peak.** A friend planted to keep a fair vote, a
  reassurance planted in the one who will be wronged, a mediator waiting at the
  table: each is a ready exit into a deal. Until the peak has played, plant
  nobody to smooth it.
- **No second push.** Plan what you do if the wrong is apologized for or undone
  in one move, before it happens.

## 3. Six engines

When there is no gas yet, build it from one of these.

### 1. Zero-sum scarcity and two kinds of fair

Two sympathetic people want one thing only one can have (a solo, a seat, a
mentor's notice), and each has a defensible idea of fairness (talent against
seniority, the group against the individual).

- **Sound! Euphonium**: a high-school concert band aims for Nationals; a strict
  new advisor holds open auditions for one trumpet solo. A fiercely talented
  first-year challenges a beloved senior who expected the part by seniority.
  Nobody is a villain; the band splits along two ideas of what's fair.
- **March Comes in Like a Lion**: a depressed teenage professional shogi player;
  every match he wins against older players crushes someone's livelihood and
  hopes.

**Here.** Announce the scarce thing where both hear of it (narrate a notice
arriving; plant the news in a third person who will mention it). Plant in each a
want for it, in their own terms. Watch whether they compete openly, sabotage or
sour.

### 2. Virtue as a coping mechanism

A character's best quality is how they survive an old wound, and it has a cost.
Put them where it stops protecting them, or hurts someone they love.

- **Fruits Basket (2019)**: an orphaned girl's relentless cheer and
  self-sacrifice turn out to be how she grieves; she can't admit a single
  selfish wish.
- **March Comes in Like a Lion**: a middle-schooler bullied for defending a
  friend hides her tears from her family, terrified of being a burden, until
  someone notices.

**Here.** No villain. An aside surfaces the lie they live by ("If I ask for help
now, they'll see I'm dead weight"); a plant makes a friend notice one tell (an
untouched lunch, a rehearsed smile) and want to ask.

### 3. Values in opposition

Two people whose beliefs contradict each other face one decision where winning
would prove the other right.

- **Monster**: a surgeon who believes every life weighs the same saved a boy who
  grew into a killer believing people are equal only in death; to stop him, the
  surgeon would have to become what the killer believes.
- **Vinland Saga, season 2**: a former warrior, now enslaved on a farm, works
  beside a farmer whose village raiders like him destroyed.
- **Haikyuu!!, season 2**: a setter who believes in one perfect, controlled play
  and a spiker who now wants to see the ball and decide for himself (case study
  below).

**Here.** Arrange a joint decision where the shortcut breaks one's principle and
serves the other's (narrate the choice arriving; plant the stake in each). Then
watch, hands off.

### 4. Unequal wants in a closed circle

Three people with wants that can't all be met, in a space they share, so small
talk carries edges.

- **Shōwa Genroku Rakugo Shinjū**: two apprentice storytellers and a geisha. One
  wants the other's companionship in the art but chooses craft over love; she
  wants him and turns to his friend out of longing and spite; the friend loves
  her and envies the other's standing. Every backstage chat crackles.

**Here.** Map the triangle on your board. Put all three in an ordinary setting
(a meal, an errand, a rehearsal: plants so each has a reason to be there), where
casual praise of one wounds another.

### 5. Small flaws that compound

No mastermind: ordinary people make small, self-protective choices (a white lie,
a debt hidden, a corner cut) that collide with rules or with each other.

- **Odd Taxi**: a taxi driver's passengers (a student chasing fame, a man hooked
  on a mobile game, a struggling comedian) make small compromises that snowball
  into a crime around his cab.
- **Breaking Bad**: every lie a teacher tells to hide his double life demands a
  bigger one.

**Here.** When a character tells a small lie or cuts a corner, log it as a seed.
Two scenes later, plant in a third person an innocent question about it, so they
must confess or double down.

### 6. A shortcut with a bill

Early on, characters enjoy a favour, a promise or a shortcut; later the cost
comes due, and resolving the crisis means giving back those gains.

- **Steins;Gate**: friends use a device that sends texts into the past, each
  fixing a personal regret; later the hero must ask each to undo their wish to
  save his friend.
- **Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood**: two brothers break the law of equivalent
  exchange trying to bring back their mother; no win in the story ever comes
  free again.

**Here.** In a building stretch, let someone get a favour or make a promise with
a quiet string attached (log it). When the clock is high, call the debt in (a
plant in whoever is owed).

## 4. Falling-outs and make-ups, studied

Each with how you would set it up here. The names in "Here" are placeholders.

### Frozen: the coronation

Two royal sisters, close as children, kept apart for years because the elder's
ice powers once hurt the younger; the elder learned to survive by hiding and
controlling everything, the younger by craving connection. At the elder's
coronation party, the younger announces she is engaged to a man she met that
day. The elder refuses her blessing and turns to leave; the younger grabs at her
and pulls off her glove, demanding to know why she has always shut her out. The
powers burst out in front of the whole court, and the elder flees.

- Why it works: two wants that are both sympathetic (control to protect others;
  connection after years shut out), an object that holds the wound (the glove),
  and a witness (the whole court), so the private fight becomes public and can't
  be taken back.
- Here: plant in the open one a decision she is sure the other will love; plant
  in the guarded one the old reason to say no; set the scene in public (plants
  for both to attend). Watch the fork: does the guarded one explain, or refuse
  and leave? Narrate nothing during the peak.

### Toy Story: the window

A cowboy toy, his boy's favourite, is displaced by a flashy new space-ranger
toy. On the night the boy may take one toy on an outing, the cowboy tries to
knock the newcomer behind the desk; it goes wrong and the ranger falls out of
the window. The other toys saw enough to believe he did it on purpose, and turn
on him.

- Why it works: the act comes from a want (to stay first in the boy's eyes), not
  malice; it is half accident, so he can defend himself honestly ("I didn't mean
  it") while still being at fault; and witnesses turn it into a verdict.
- Here: scarcity first (plant in each the want for the one thing; announce the
  chance by narration). The act is the cowboy's; let the plant make him want to
  keep it, not to hurt anyone. A misunderstanding is a legitimate match when the
  gas is real: narrate what the witnesses see, not what happened.

### Haikyuu!!: the quick

A setter and a spiker built a famous fast attack: the spiker swings with his
eyes shut and the setter puts the ball exactly where his hand will be. After a
loss to a wall of tall blockers, the spiker wants to see the ball and choose his
own shot. The setter refuses: their attack is already perfect. They argue after
practice until it turns into a scuffle a teammate has to break up; they train
apart, each stubborn, until each finds a way to meet the other.

- Why it works: both are right about something they care about (craft, control
  over their own play); it comes from the same goal, to win; and the make-up is
  an act (a new play they build), not a speech.
- Here: values in opposition. Plant the new want in one ("I'm done swinging
  blind; next time I choose the shot") and the old belief in the other ("we
  don't fix what works"). Put them in the shared task (a practice, a rehearsal,
  a job). The make-up is a scene where each gives something in the work itself.

### Avatar: The Last Airbender: the uncle in the catacombs, and the tent

An exiled prince and the uncle who raised him in exile and loved him as a son.
Offered his way home, the prince sides with his sister against his friends' new
allies, and his uncle is taken prisoner. When the prince later visits him in his
cell, the uncle won't speak to him at all. Only much later, having walked away
from everything he chose, does the prince come to his uncle's tent ready to be
refused, and apologize; the uncle embraces him: he was never angry, only sad.

- Why it works: the betrayal comes from the prince's deepest want (to be
  welcomed home), not cruelty; the distance is silence, not shouting; the
  make-up costs the prince everything he had chosen, and happens because he
  expects nothing.
- Here: for the distance, the wronged one's silence is a strong choice (plant "I
  have nothing to say to her until she knows what she did"). For the make-up,
  plant the apology in the one who must give way as something that will cost
  them (giving back, saying it in front of others); don't plant forgiveness in
  the other; let them decide.

### The Banshees of Inisherin: the end of a friendship

On a small island, a man abruptly tells his lifelong drinking friend he no
longer wants to be friends: he is getting old and wants to spend his time on
music, not on dull talk. The friend, kind and simple, can't accept it and keeps
trying, and each attempt makes it worse.

- Why it works: unequal wants (one wants meaning, the other companionship) in a
  closed place where they can't avoid each other; neither is a villain; the hurt
  one's persistence is the escalation.
- Here: a falling-out can start with no wrong at all, only a want one of them
  pursues at the other's expense. Plant the want in one, plainly; let the other
  be hurt by it. Use the small place: plants for both to keep crossing paths.

### Lady Bird: the car

A teenage girl and her mother drive home from a college tour, crying together
over a novel on tape; within a minute they are arguing about her wanting a
college far away the family can't afford, and she throws herself out of the
moving car.

- Why it works: love and anger in one scene, the turn on a single sentence about
  money, and an act that ends the argument with a statement.
- Here: enter late (start the scene when both are already somewhere they can't
  leave easily). The want (escape) and the wound (we can't afford you) should
  already be planted; the scene only needs the subject raised.

## 5. The register of a clash

Not every fight is a shouting match. Match the register to the characters'
masks:

- **Polite deflection and tells**: a proud, witty senior who refuses to depend
  on anyone (Sound! Euphonium) never cries or asks for help; the most her pride
  allows is casually inviting a junior over to "study". With such characters,
  look for what they do instead of saying it.
- **Two worlds that bleed**: a young shogi player's ruthless match hall and the
  warm family table where he is cared for (March Comes in Like a Lion); friction
  comes when habits from one leak into the other.
- **Loud**: a character whose mask is bluster will shout; one whose mask is
  control will go cold. A plant or an aside written in their own register is the
  one they act on.
