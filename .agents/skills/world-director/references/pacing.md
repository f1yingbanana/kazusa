# Pacing

Stories breathe. When to push, when to hold, how to rest without losing the
thread, and how to build an arc with no villain.

Contents: 1. Four states · 2. Your rhythm across days · 3. Breathers inside a
serious story · 4. Two structures

## 1. Four states

Track each thread (and the world as a whole) through four states, after the way
a game's AI director paces tension and rest:

| State    | What it is for                                                   | Your posture                                         | Your tools                                                           |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Build-up | Routines, seeds, a tightening clock, small friction accumulating | Light, deliberate nudges                             | Plants, a third person, an early deadline narrated or planted        |
| Peak     | The confrontation, reveal or irreversible choice, live           | Hands off: let them collide                          | Watching only; an aside only if the scene dodges the stakes entirely |
| Fade     | The aftermath: shock, silence, regret, fragile relief            | Take the pressure off at once; let it sit            | Watch how they lick their wounds; no new crises                      |
| Rest     | Recovery, humour, ordinary life, a secondary character's turn    | Leave the main thread alone, or run a small breather | A gentle setting, or move your watches to another thread             |

One or two peaks in a sim day is plenty.

## 2. Your rhythm across days

Pick a rhythm that suits the world, like a game's storytellers do:

- **Steady escalation**: waves of build-up, peak and rest, each a little higher.
  For a structured story with a clear request.
- **Long breath, rare spike**: long peaceful stretches where routines and
  closeness grow, broken by rare turning points. For slice-of-life worlds.
- **Crossroads**: several independent threads running at once that sometimes
  collide, always for reasons inside the characters, never at random.

## 3. Breathers inside a serious story

A quiet episode makes a serious one hit harder, if it lifts the plot's speed
without lifting its feeling.

- **The campfire before the battle** (Attack on Titan, the night before the
  final battle): enemies from both sides of a long war share a stew around a
  fire, and the quiet forces old guilt into the open, so their alliance is
  earned by morning. _Here_: before a deadline, put reluctant allies in a
  domestic task (cooking, waiting out a storm: plants for both to be there, a
  narrated storm). One aside surfaces a shared memory.
- **A day off, then a re-hook** (Avatar: The Last Airbender, "The Tales of Ba
  Sing Se"): mid-crisis, six small stories: a spa day, an exiled prince's
  awkward tender date that shows he could be happy (so his later betrayal hurts
  more), an old man's day of kindnesses ending in grief for his son; the last
  vignette ends on a giant footprint in the mud that snaps back to the main
  plot. _Here_: a full day with no emergencies, and at the very end one quiet
  breadcrumb (a name overheard, a letter) that points back.
- **A comic mirror before the storm** (Avatar, "The Ember Island Players"): the
  heroes watch a ridiculous stage play about their own adventures; it gets
  something painfully right and ends by staging their deaths, right before the
  finale. _Here_: a festival or a rumour mill retells the cast's past wrongly;
  they laugh, until one detail is too accurate.
- **The bottle episode** (Breaking Bad, "Fly"): two partners spend a whole
  episode trapped in their lab chasing one fly; the slapstick is a stand-in for
  a guilt one of them almost confesses. _Here_: lock two people with a heavy
  secret into a mundane, slightly absurd task (a lost key, a stockroom count).
- **Stillness with a horizon** (Cowboy Bebop's domestic banter; Frieren's quiet
  stops after the hero's quest is over; Vinland Saga's peaceful farm with an
  army on its way): ordinary happy moments under a known coming threat gain ache
  without any fight.

A breather strengthens a story when it comes after a peak or just before an
irreversible one, shows who they would be in peace, lets buried feelings surface
quietly and ends with a gentle re-hook. It weakens it mid-emergency, or when it
erases established hurt overnight, or changes nothing.

## 4. Two structures

- **Three acts** (setup, escalating confrontation, climax and resolution): when
  there are opposing goals, a deadline and real stakes. Fights, rivalries,
  competitions.
- **Kishōtenketsu** (introduction, development, an unexpected turn, a new
  state): momentum from a change of view rather than conflict. Introduce a
  routine, deepen it, drop in something unrelated (a visitor, a memory, a
  parallel event) that makes them see the routine differently. For friendships
  forming, slice-of-life, quiet character episodes.
